Your mission is to help people's dream of playing an instrument come true. Most people who start don't make it through the first year, so the dream usually falls apart in the first few weeks. That's a design problem, and it's the one I'd most like to work on with you.

So instead of a cover letter, here is a small, end-to-end piece of work. I studied Musopia and its products, framed one problem, designed a concept for it, and planned how to test it. The concept is built around what JustinGuitar already does well: Justin's teaching, the Practice Assistant, One Minute Changes and the Instant Play Method.

I've only seen the product from the outside, so treat everything here as a set of hypotheses for us to question together.

What makes it work

The Instant Play Method uses simplified chords, strum patterns, tempo control and lyrics, so you play a real song in minutes. Justin's teaching is calm, trusted and kind. The pedagogy is real: One Minute Changes gives you one clear number to beat. Freemium subscriptions fund it all.

There's a brand that knows its voice too: "ignite the music in you."

02 · The problem

Wanting to play is easy. Staying is the hard part.

75%

of people would like to play an instrument (Musopia)

90%

of new guitarists quit within 12 months (Fender, 2019)

Fender's CEO put it plainly: "We don't have a problem attracting new entrants. We have a retention issue." For a subscription app, the first weeks decide everything, including trial conversion, renewals, ratings and word of mouth.

Beginners quit for reasons that are physical and emotional before they are about content. Their fingers hurt, progress feels invisible, the songs they wanted feel far away, and one missed day turns into a missed week.

Motivation across a beginner's first month

Hypothesis to validate

How might we…

…make a beginner's invisible progress visible, so the hard first weeks feel worth it?

…and…

…keep the song that made them start in sight from day one?

×2

"If we could reduce the abandonment rate by just 10 percent, we could double the size of the industry." (Andy Mooney, Fender). For Musopia, the same lever drives subscription lifetime value. That's why retention in the first month is the business case for this concept, and not just a UX nicety.

03 · Principles & flow

Five rules for the first month.

1

Song first

Every exercise is a step toward the song that made you start. The path is the curriculum, wrapped in the user's own reason.

2

Make progress visible

Calluses and muscle memory are invisible. Changes per minute are not. Measure the things that are improving and show them.

3

Rhythm, not streaks

Set a weekly target with rest days built in. Sore fingers need breaks, and a broken chain shouldn't make anyone feel like a failure.

4

No dead ends

A hard chord should never stop the music. Instant Play always offers a simpler version, and the real one stays one tap away.

5

Sound like Justin

Every message is warm, specific and honest. "Your fingers remember more than you think" beats "Don't lose your streak!"

The journey, end to end

Questions I'd bring to the team

Kala already listens to playing. Can that detection be reused for chord changes on guitar?

Where does the paywall sit compared with the first song unlock today, and what do the cohorts show?

Can why-songs come from a licence-stable list, so a goal never disappears?

How much of the first month happens on the website instead of the app, and how does that affect sync?

05 · How we'd know it works

Ship it as experiments, not as a bet.

#We believe…We'll know if…
H1Choosing a why song during onboarding makes the first month feel personalD7 retention and the share of users who finish their first session go up for new installs
H2Listening One Minute Changes plus a progress curve make improvement feel realMore practice days in weeks 2–4, and more users reach 30 changes per minute
H3A weekly rhythm with kind comebacks keeps more people than daily streaksMore lapsed users come back within 14 days, and fewer turn off notifications
H4Feeling an early song unlocked moment makes paying feel worth itTrial-to-paid conversion and month-2 renewals go up

North star

Learners who play their first full song within 30 days. This lines up value for users with revenue.

Guardrails

Onboarding completion, mic-permission drop-off, refund rate, store rating, crash-free sessions.

Rollout

Weeks 1–2 · Discover

Interview 8–10 lapsed and active beginners, review cohort data around days 3–10, and set the D7 and D30 baselines.

Weeks 3–4 · Prototype & test

Run a clickable Figma prototype with 5–6 beginners holding a real guitar. Test the mic accuracy of chord-change detection with engineering.

Weeks 5–8 · Ship H1 + H3

Start with the cheapest changes, the onboarding question and the weekly rhythm, as an A/B test on new installs in one market.

Weeks 9+ · Ship H2 + H4

Add listening One Minute Changes and the unlock moment, then iterate on the cohort curves.

Events to instrument

why_song_selected · session_started{source} · omc_completed{pair, count, input} · path_chord_mastered · song_unlocked · rhythm_goal_changed · comeback_shown/accepted

Usability test script (weeks 3–4), with a guitar in hand

Task 1

"Set the app up to learn a song you love."

Pass: finds or swaps a why song in under 60 s, and understands the time estimate.

Task 2

"Do today's practice."

Pass: starts without help and can say why each step is there.

Task 3

"Do a one-minute chord change."

Pass: trusts the count. We log the gap between mic and observed counts and how often people fall back to tapping.

Task 4

"You skipped a week. Open the app."

Pass: describes the feeling as "welcome", not "guilty", and finishes the 2-minute re-entry.

New components

AEG

Each component has defined states, including empty, loading, offline and mic-denied, before it's handed off.

Notes for a Unity build

→

Export tokens as JSON from Figma variables so Unity UI and native screens read one source of truth.

→

Spec motion in ms with easing curves (unlock: 600 ms, ease-out-back). The waveform is one looping asset.

→

Agree on the chord-detection confidence threshold with engineering. Under the threshold, don't count, don't scold.

→

Give every surface a sync status, which responds to the most common store complaint.

→

Run accessibility checks: AA contrast on dark surfaces, Dynamic Type up to 200%, and a tap-to-count alternative to the mic.
